Most people ask questions to confirm what they already think.

The question is phrased as a question but it's really a statement in disguise. *Isn't it true that users want X?* *Don't you think the problem is Y?* The answer is almost predetermined — the asker just wants the other person to ratify the conclusion.

I spent a lot of time doing this before I realized how much I was losing.

The best user interviews I've run started with questions I genuinely didn't know the answer to. Not strategic neutrality — actual uncertainty. *Walk me through what happens when you open the app in the morning.* Not: *When you open the app, do you usually go to the dashboard first?* The second question narrows. The first one opens.

There's a technique I've started calling **the question before the question.** Before I ask what I think I want to know, I ask myself: *what would have to be true for this question to matter?* If I'm asking "do users find the navigation confusing," what has to be true is that navigation is the bottleneck. Is it? Maybe. But what if the real issue is that users don't know what they're trying to do in the first place, and that's why navigation seems hard? Asking the question-before-the-question surfaces the assumption.

The best questions I've been asked were the ones I'd never thought about. Someone asked me once: *What problem are you afraid this product won't solve?* That stopped me cold. I'd been so focused on the problem I believed the product would solve that I hadn't articulated the fear underneath it. That one question changed the direction of the roadmap.

Good questions do a few things: they create space for the other person to surprise you, they reveal assumptions you didn't know you were making, and they make the asker seem curious rather than clever.

The last one matters. Clever questions are interesting. Curious questions are useful.

The goal isn't to demonstrate what you know. It's to find out what you don't.
