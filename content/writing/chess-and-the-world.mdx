I came to chess late. Not childhood-prodigy late — I mean I started playing seriously at 22 and within four months had gone from 1000 to 1600. People ask how. The honest answer is: I got very good at losing.

Not comfortable with it. Not cheerful about it. Good at it, the way a surgeon is good at delivering bad news — it's a skill, not a personality trait.

Here's what I mean. Most players at 1000 lose and think about what their opponent did. Most players at 1600 lose and think about what they did. The shift is small and enormous at the same time. The first approach is passive — you're a spectator to your own defeat. The second approach is active — you're a student of your own failures.

**The game doesn't care about your intentions.** This was the first thing chess taught me. I could have a brilliant plan — beautiful, logical, sophisticated — and if it rested on one faulty assumption, the plan was garbage. No partial credit. No "but I was trying to do X." The board is objective. Either your position is good or it isn't.

I've tried to bring this into product work. Plans look great on whiteboards. Users don't care what you were trying to do. The product either works for them or it doesn't.

**Most mistakes happen before you think they happen.** In chess, the move that loses the game is rarely the losing move. It's the move three turns earlier that created the losing position. By the time you see the problem, the problem is already done.

This is true of most things. The product decision that tanks a launch was usually made six months before the launch. The relationship that fails in year three started failing in year one. The mistake you can see isn't the mistake that matters.

**You have to play the board in front of you.** Not the board you wish you had. Not the board your opponent should have given you. The actual current position, with all its limitations and ugliness.

This sounds obvious until you're in a losing position and every impulse is to pretend it isn't as bad as it is, or to look for a miracle that the position doesn't support. The players who improve are the ones who can look at a bad position clearly and find the best available move, not the best imaginable move.

**Skill compounds weirdly.** The difference between 1000 and 1200 is pattern recognition. The difference between 1200 and 1400 is calculation. The difference between 1400 and 1600 is positional understanding. Each level requires a different kind of learning. You can't shortcut your way up — you have to build each layer.

I think about this when I watch people try to shortcut capability. You can sometimes fake a level above you. You can rarely perform at it. The work is non-negotiable.

I'm still playing. Still losing matches I shouldn't. Still learning.
