The best feedback I've ever gotten came from people who weren't trying to give me feedback.

A surgeon I was watching during OR research mentioned offhand that the software "always does the weird thing during the quiet part." He wasn't filing a bug report. He wasn't trying to help me redesign anything. He was just narrating his experience to fill the silence. But that sentence — *the weird thing during the quiet part* — was more useful than a hundred survey responses.

I've started thinking about every interaction as a potential learning moment. Not in a transactional way — I'm not mining conversations for data points. It's more that I try to stay in a kind of listening posture even when I'm supposed to be doing something else.

The teacher-student framing is one I return to a lot. There's a version of it that's hierarchical: the teacher is the expert, the student is the novice, knowledge flows in one direction. That version is almost always wrong in practice.

The better version: **teacher and student are roles, not identities.** In any given moment, you're one or the other depending on what's being learned. The PM who has been in the space for ten years is the teacher when it comes to market dynamics. They're the student when a 23-year-old user shows them how they actually use the product.

The mistake I see most often is people who get stuck in one role. The expert who can't be taught. The novice who can't teach. Both are losing.

The hardest part of being a good student is that it requires a kind of temporary ego suspension. You have to be willing to be wrong, visibly, in front of someone. That's uncomfortable. It's also the only way to learn anything real.

The hardest part of being a good teacher is that it requires patience for the pace of someone else's understanding. You have to meet people where they are, not where you wish they were. That's also uncomfortable.

Most of the people I admire are students first. Not in the sense that they're deferential or uncertain — they're often neither. But they have an orientation toward learning that doesn't shut off when they become the expert. They still find things interesting. They still get surprised.

That's the goal, I think. Not to stop being a student when you become good at something. But to stay curious even when — especially when — you're the one in the room who knows the most.
