title: "The line between stubbornness and resilience"
date: "2025-09-01"

There's a version of persistence that gets you killed and a version that gets you through. From the outside, they look the same.

The stubborn person and the resilient person are both refusing to quit. Both are doing uncomfortable things. Both are ignoring people who say it can't be done. The difference isn't visible in the behavior — it's in the orientation.

Stubbornness is attachment to a specific path. Resilience is commitment to a specific outcome.

I've been both. I know what stubbornness feels like from the inside: a kind of tightness, a refusal to update, a sense that changing course would be an admission of failure. You tell yourself it's conviction. It might be, for a while. But at some point the evidence accumulates and you realize you've been protecting the plan instead of pursuing the goal.

Resilience feels different. There's a looseness to it. You're not attached to *how* you get there — only to *getting there*. That looseness lets you absorb information. Pivot. Take a hit, recalibrate, keep moving.

The test I've started using: **am I defending a decision or solving a problem?**

If I'm defending a decision, I'm probably being stubborn. The energy is backward-facing — justifying what I already did. If I'm solving a problem, the energy is forward-facing — using what I know, including what I've learned from being wrong, to figure out the next move.

This isn't a perfect heuristic. Sometimes decisions need defending. Sometimes the critics are wrong and you're right and you should stay the course. But it's a useful pressure test, especially when you're tired and the path forward is murky.

Most things worth doing will have a moment where they look like they're failing. That moment is where stubbornness and resilience diverge. One of them says: *I will not change because changing is losing.* The other says: *I will change whatever needs changing to win.*

Be the second one.
