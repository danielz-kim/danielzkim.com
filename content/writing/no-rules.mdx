title: "No rules"
date: "2025-01-10"

excerpt: "Rules are codified judgment calls from a context that may not be yours."
---

Every rule is someone's judgment call that got written down.

Most rules exist because something went wrong once. Someone made a decision, it blew up, and the post-mortem produced a rule: *we don't do that anymore.* The rule made sense in context. The context moved on. The rule stayed.

I'm not arguing against rules. I'm arguing against inherited rules — rules you follow without knowing why they exist, rules whose original context has evaporated, rules that are more about institutional memory than current reality.

The useful question isn't *what are the rules?* It's *why does this rule exist?* If you can answer that clearly, you can apply the rule intelligently. You can recognize when the underlying conditions are present and when they're not. You can know when to follow the rule and when the rule is the problem.

This comes up constantly in product work. Conventions exist for reasons — dark patterns are bad because they erode trust, lengthy onboarding is bad because time-to-value matters, simplicity is good because cognitive load is real. But conventions are not laws. The question is always: does this principle apply to this context?

The companies that do genuinely new things usually start by asking why the existing approach exists, not just what it is. If the answer is "because that's how everyone does it," that's not a reason. That's an opportunity.

The flip side: some rules exist because someone tried the alternative and it went badly, and you just don't know about it. Chesterton's fence. Before you tear down a fence, understand why it was built. You might still tear it down. But do it with information.

The balance I try to hold: enough respect for rules to understand their origin, enough irreverence to question whether that origin still applies.

Most rules are fine. A few of them are the thing standing between you and something better.
