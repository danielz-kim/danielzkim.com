<Callout>
  Promaxo makes a portable MRI system designed for in-office and intraoperative use. My role: lead the UX research and redesign of the software interface surgeons and technologists interact with during live procedures. The outcome was a redesigned workflow that reduced intraoperative software errors by 60% and unlocked $440k in annual recurring revenue from previously lost or delayed cases.
</Callout>

## The brief

Promaxo had a working MRI device. The hardware was impressive — portable, room-temperature magnet, genuinely novel. The software interface was... not. It had been built by engineers for engineers. The people actually using it in operating rooms were surgeons, radiology techs, and OR nurses.

Nobody had watched them use it.

## What I did first

I got into an OR.

Not to redesign anything. To watch. To understand what a live procedure actually looks like when you add a portable MRI to the room. The sterile field constraints, the workflow handoffs, the moments of cognitive load.

<VisualPlaceholder label="Field research notes — OR observation, Promaxo MRI workflow" />

What I found: users had developed shadow workflows to compensate for the interface's gaps. There was a sticky note on one system reminding techs which menu sequence to follow because the labeled one was wrong. A surgeon had memorized an undocumented keyboard shortcut that saved him three taps during the critical imaging phase.

These workarounds weren't bugs in the user. They were signals in the system.

<PullQuote>
  Every workaround is a feature request the user gave up filing.
</PullQuote>

## The research

I ran contextual inquiry sessions with six clinical users across three sites. Key questions:

- Where in the workflow are you making decisions you're not confident about?
- What do you do when the system doesn't behave as expected?
- What's the single thing you wish the software knew about your job?

I mapped every answer to a moment in the workflow. Clustered by phase. Prioritized by frequency and severity.

<VisualPlaceholder label="Affinity map — clinical workflow pain points by phase" />

Three major failure modes emerged:

1. **The imaging phase handoff** — the transfer from surgeon control to tech control was ambiguous. The interface didn't signal whose responsibility the system was at any given moment.
2. **Error recovery** — when something went wrong, the error state was a wall of text that told you what happened but not what to do next. Techs were improvising.
3. **Scan parameter confirmation** — surgeons were confirming scan settings they couldn't interpret, creating a compliance checkbox moment that added stress without adding safety.

## The redesign

I prioritized the three failure modes and designed solutions for each.

**Handoff clarity:** Added a persistent role indicator and a handoff confirmation step. Fifteen seconds of friction that eliminated a category of error.

**Error recovery:** Rewrote every error state as a two-part message: what happened + what to do next. Paired with a documentation link for edge cases.

**Scan parameters:** Redesigned the confirmation screen to surface only the parameters the surgeon needed to verify, with plain-language descriptions. Pushed technical parameters to an expandable secondary view for the tech.

## What I learned

Clinical UX is not about making things simpler. It's about making the right things visible at the right moment to the right person. The OR has a protocol culture — users want systems that respect the protocol, not systems that replace their judgment.

The $440k outcome wasn't from a grand redesign. It was from three targeted interventions, each rooted in a specific observed behavior. That's what field research buys you that a user survey never will.
