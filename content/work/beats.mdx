## The research question

Beats products are worn by athletes. They're in every locker room, on every track, in every gym. But the product had been designed around a generalized consumer use case — commuting, casual listening, working from home.

The question I was brought in to answer: *Are athletes actually a distinct use case? Or are they just consumers who happen to sweat?*

## What the research showed

I ran a mixed-methods study: 24 one-on-one interviews across amateur runners, competitive cyclists, and professional team-sport athletes (NBA, NFL), plus a quantitative survey of 400 respondents self-identifying as regular athletic music listeners.

The finding was unambiguous. **Athletes are a distinct use case.**

Three insight clusters stood out:

**Cluster 1: Situational awareness vs. isolation is not a binary.**

Athletes didn't want full isolation or full ambient — they wanted dynamic control. A runner wants to hear traffic. A cyclist wants to hear a pace call. A basketball player warming up wants music; the same player in the final two minutes of a game does not. The product treated this as a toggle. Users treated it as a spectrum.

**Cluster 2: Physical durability signals psychological safety.**

Athletes reported that visible wear on their devices — scratches, dents, cable fraying — made them *less likely* to use them during training. Not because the product didn't work, but because they were protecting it. A product positioned for athletes needs to be one users feel comfortable destroying.

**Cluster 3: The locker room use case is underserved.**

The highest-intensity moment of music use in sports isn't training. It's the 20 minutes before competition. Shared spaces, high stakes, social and private simultaneously. No product on the market was designed for that moment. Beats could own it.

## How it moved the roadmap

I presented the three clusters with evidence and prioritization rationale to the product team. The situational awareness insight directly influenced the next-generation adaptive audio feature brief. The durability insight went into materials review. The locker room insight became the basis for a marketing brief I'm told is still referenced internally.

I can't share more specifics. But the research moved things.

## What I think about this work

Consumer electronics research at this scale is humbling. You're studying products people have strong feelings about, in moments that matter to them. Getting that right requires a certain amount of ego suspension — you have to let the data tell you something you didn't expect, and then fight for it.

The insight that mattered most — the locker room use case — wasn't in my original research plan. It emerged from a question I asked offhand in the third interview. That's why you do qualitative work. You don't know what you don't know until someone tells you.
